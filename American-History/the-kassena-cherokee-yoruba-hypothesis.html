The Kassena–Yoruba–Cherokee Hypothesis: A Transatlantic Population, Linguistic, Historical, and Reparations Inquiry Abstract This paper advances and examines a controversial historical hypothesis: that populations ancestral to the Kassena and Yoruba peoples of West Africa participated in pre-Columbian transatlantic exploration and settlement, that these populations contributed ancestry and cultural influence to peoples who subsequently became associated with the Cherokee, and that, if such an ancestral relationship could be demonstrated, the historical and legal consequences could include claims for recognition, reparations, land, political rights, and government benefits. The hypothesis is presented as a research proposition rather than as an established historical fact. Contemporary archaeology, population genetics, and historical linguistics do not presently establish that the Kassena or Yoruba discovered North America or that the Cherokee descend substantially from those populations. Nevertheless, the proposition can be subjected to systematic testing through comparative genetics, linguistics, archaeology, anthropology, population history, and legal scholarship. A related premise concerns human pigmentation. Ancient-DNA research demonstrates that pigmentation in prehistoric Europe was more complex and variable than simplistic modern racial categories imply, and that the frequency of pigmentation-associated genetic variants changed through prehistoric population movements and natural selection. This demonstrates the broader methodological importance of distinguishing modern racial appearance from ancient population history. The central research question is therefore: Could a previously unrecognized West African contribution to the ethnogenesis of a North American Indigenous population be demonstrated through convergent genetic, linguistic, archaeological, and historical evidence, and, if so, what consequences would such a finding have for contemporary claims concerning identity, recognition, reparations, land, and government benefits? I. Introduction The conventional history of the Americas describes the ancestors of Indigenous American peoples as deriving primarily from ancient populations connected to northeastern Asia who entered and subsequently diversified throughout the Americas. This broad model is supported by extensive archaeological and genetic evidence. At the same time, the history of human migration is considerably more complicated than modern racial categories suggest. Human populations have migrated, mixed, adapted, exchanged technologies, and experienced demographic replacement for thousands of years. Physical appearance is therefore an unreliable standalone indicator of ancestry. This observation raises a broader historical question: Could populations originating in West Africa have crossed the Atlantic before European colonization and subsequently contributed to an Indigenous population of North America? The present study focuses this question specifically on two West African populations—the Kassena and Yoruba—and the Cherokee. The proposed hypothesis can be stated as follows: H₁: One or more populations ancestral to the Kassena and/or Yoruba participated in pre-Columbian Atlantic voyages, established contact or settlements in the Americas, and contributed genetically, linguistically, culturally, or demographically to populations ancestral to the Cherokee. A stronger version of the hypothesis would assert substantial biological ancestry. A weaker version would require only demonstrable pre-Columbian contact. These propositions must be distinguished because evidence for contact does not necessarily establish demographic replacement or biological descent. II. Human Skin Pigmentation and the Problem of Modern Racial Categories One reason ancient population history is difficult to reconstruct is that modern observers frequently treat physical appearance as though it were a permanent marker of population identity. It is not. Human pigmentation is influenced by numerous genetic variants and has changed repeatedly as human populations migrated into different environments. Ancient populations in northern Europe demonstrate this principle particularly well. Ancient-DNA studies have identified substantial variation in pigmentation-related alleles among prehistoric European populations. Consequently, the statement that modern northern Europeans have always possessed their present-day pigmentation profile is historically inaccurate. This has an important methodological implication for the present study. A person's skin color cannot establish whether that person is genetically descended from Africa, Europe, Asia, or the Americas. Likewise, the presence of dark or light pigmentation among ancient individuals cannot independently establish geographic origin. Genomic ancestry, archaeological context, chronology, and population genetics must therefore be examined together. The lesson is not that prehistoric Europeans were necessarily Africans. The lesson is that phenotype cannot substitute for population history. III. The Kassena The Kassena are a West African people primarily associated with the present-day Ghana–Burkina Faso region. Their language, Kasem, belongs to the Gur branch of the Atlantic-Congo language family. The Kassena therefore provide an important comparative population for any proposed West African–American migration hypothesis. A serious investigation would examine: Kassena population genetics; ancient DNA from the region where available; Y-chromosome lineages; mitochondrial lineages; autosomal ancestry; archaeological culture; agricultural practices; metallurgical traditions; religious and ceremonial practices; oral traditions; maritime knowledge; linguistic relationships. The investigation should distinguish modern Kassena identity from ancient populations that may have contributed to the formation of present-day Kassena communities. This distinction is essential because modern ethnic groups are not necessarily identical to their Bronze Age, Iron Age, or medieval predecessors. IV. The Yoruba The Yoruba constitute one of the largest historically documented populations of West Africa and are primarily associated with southwestern Nigeria and neighboring regions. Yoruba belongs to the Atlantic-Congo language family. Yoruba civilization developed extensive urban, artistic, political, religious, and commercial traditions. A proposed transatlantic-contact hypothesis would therefore have to determine whether there is evidence that a population ancestral to the Yoruba possessed the technological and demographic capacity to participate in Atlantic voyages before European colonization. The existence of maritime capability is not itself proof of transatlantic migration. Instead, the research question would be whether archaeological and historical evidence identifies a plausible chain connecting West African maritime activity to the Americas. V. The Cherokee The Cherokee are an Indigenous people of the southeastern United States. Cherokee is classified within the Iroquoian language family, specifically Southern Iroquoian. This classification creates one of the most significant challenges to the proposed hypothesis. If the Cherokee were substantially descended from Yoruba or Kassena populations, one would need to explain how a West African population speaking an Atlantic-Congo language could become associated with an Iroquoian-speaking population. Several mechanisms could theoretically produce such a result: language replacement; elite language adoption; population fusion; demographic assimilation; small-scale migration; cultural adoption; bilingualism followed by language loss; incorporation into an existing Indigenous population. Consequently, the absence of obvious Yoruba or Kassena vocabulary in Cherokee would not logically eliminate every conceivable form of historical contact. However, it would make a claim of direct linguistic descent considerably more difficult. VI. Comparative Linguistics The linguistic portion of this hypothesis is particularly suitable for systematic testing. The three languages should be compared using the historical-comparative method rather than by selecting words that merely sound alike. The dataset should include at least 1,000 lexical items. These should include: pronouns; numerals; kinship terminology; body parts; animals; plants; geographical terms; household vocabulary; agricultural vocabulary; tools; weapons; verbs; adjectives; grammatical particles; demonstratives; interrogatives; basic nouns. Grammar as Evidence Vocabulary is not the only linguistic evidence. Grammar may provide even stronger evidence because grammatical systems can preserve historical relationships that ordinary vocabulary does not. The proposed investigation should compare: Cherokee pronominal prefixes; verbal agreement; aspect; tense; plurality; noun incorporation; derivational morphology; clause structure. Yoruba pronouns; tense; aspect; negation; serial verbs; noun phrases; tone; relative clauses. Kasem pronouns; noun morphology; verbal morphology; tone; aspect; negation; syntax. If the hypothesis were correct, researchers would search for statistically significant structural correspondences. The strongest result would not be: "These words sound similar." The strongest result would be: "These languages repeatedly preserve the same ancestral forms through predictable phonological transformations and corresponding grammatical structures." That is the standard required for a convincing historical linguistic argument. VIII. Genetics Genetic evidence represents the most direct test of the biological component of the hypothesis. The central question is: Do securely dated pre-Columbian individuals associated with ancestral Cherokee populations contain genetic ancestry best explained by West African populations? This requires extraordinary care. Modern Cherokee individuals cannot by themselves answer the question because post-Columbian population movements introduced European and African ancestry into Indigenous American populations. The ideal dataset would therefore consist of: securely dated ancient DNA; archaeological provenance; sufficient genome coverage; contamination controls; multiple individuals; appropriate West African comparative populations; appropriate Indigenous American comparative populations; chronological separation between pre-Columbian and post-Columbian samples. The analysis should examine: Autosomal DNA Would pre-Columbian Cherokee-associated genomes contain measurable West African ancestry? Y chromosome Would paternal lineages associated with West Africa occur in pre-Columbian Cherokee-associated males? Mitochondrial DNA Would maternal lineages associated with West Africa occur in those populations? Genome-wide analysis Would statistical models consistently identify West African ancestry as the best explanation? A particularly important requirement would be replication. One anomalous individual would not necessarily establish a population-level migration. A repeated pattern across numerous securely dated individuals would be substantially more significant. IX. Archaeological Evidence Genetics should not be examined in isolation. A genuine transatlantic migration should ideally leave archaeological traces. Researchers should investigate: ceramics; metallurgy; agricultural practices; architecture; burial practices; religious objects; artistic motifs; maritime technology; tools; domesticated plants; domesticated animals; trade goods. The chronological question is crucial. An artifact that arrived in North America after 1492 cannot demonstrate pre-Columbian contact. Likewise, an artifact whose provenance is uncertain cannot carry the same evidentiary weight as an artifact discovered in a securely dated archaeological layer. The strongest evidence would therefore consist of: securely dated, independently authenticated artifacts displaying technological or cultural characteristics demonstrably connected to West Africa and occurring in a pre-Columbian archaeological context. X. Botanical Evidence Plants offer another potentially powerful method. If West African populations established sustained communities in North America before European contact, researchers could examine whether crops or agricultural practices moved across the Atlantic. Potential candidates would include: crop species; varieties; cultivation methods; processing techniques; agricultural terminology. However, botanical evidence must be interpreted cautiously because plants can spread through numerous mechanisms. A plant's presence does not automatically establish human transatlantic migration. The strongest botanical evidence would involve: a geographically unusual crop; secure pre-Columbian dating; genetic continuity with an African population; evidence of deliberate cultivation; multiple archaeological contexts. XI. Maritime Feasibility The hypothesis also requires a plausible mechanism for transatlantic movement. The Atlantic Ocean is enormous, but prehistoric and ancient peoples possessed sophisticated maritime technologies. The relevant question is therefore not simply: "Could a boat cross the Atlantic?" but: "Is there evidence that a particular West African population possessed the maritime technology, navigational knowledge, organizational capacity, and demographic incentives necessary to accomplish such a migration?" This distinction prevents the argument from relying on mere possibility. A voyage being physically possible does not demonstrate that it occurred. XII. Oral Traditions Oral traditions should also be examined. Researchers could compare: migration narratives; origin traditions; foreigner traditions; stories concerning ocean crossings; accounts of strangers arriving from distant lands; geographic myths; cultural heroes; traditions concerning unusual physical appearance. However, oral traditions must be dated and contextualized. A modern interpretation of an ancient oral tradition cannot automatically be treated as a contemporaneous historical record. The proper approach is comparative: When was the tradition recorded? Who recorded it? What was the community's historical context? Does the tradition have multiple independent versions? Does archaeology corroborate it? XIII. The Progenitor Question The term "progenitor" requires particular care. There are at least three possible meanings: Biological progenitors A population directly contributes genetic ancestry to a later population. Cultural progenitors A population contributes institutions, technologies, religious traditions, agricultural systems, or cultural practices. Ethnogenetic progenitors Two or more populations merge and contribute to the formation of a later ethnic identity. The hypothesis could therefore be tested at multiple levels. It is possible, in principle, for historical contact to exist without substantial biological ancestry. Conversely, substantial biological ancestry could exist without preserving the ancestral population's language. Therefore: Genetic ancestry, cultural ancestry, linguistic ancestry, and political identity must not be treated as interchangeable concepts. XIV. The Reparations Question If future research established that a West African population participated substantially in the pre-Columbian formation of a Cherokee-associated population, a second question would emerge: Would descendants of the relevant West African population possess a contemporary claim against the United States? That question cannot be answered solely through historical genetics. Reparations depend on legal relationships. Relevant questions would include: Who suffered the legally cognizable injury? Who caused it? When did the injury occur? Which government possessed jurisdiction? Were treaties involved? Did a recognized political community possess land rights? Were those rights subsequently violated? What statutes apply? What treaties apply? Who possesses standing? What tribal citizenship rules apply? What precedent exists for monetary or land-based remedies? A historical discovery would therefore potentially alter the factual premises of a legal argument without automatically establishing a particular remedy. XV. Reservations and Land A similar distinction applies to reservations. A reservation is not simply a geographic location inhabited historically by a population. Modern reservations are connected to particular legal and political relationships involving tribal governments, treaties, federal recognition, statutes, executive actions, and judicial decisions. Consequently, even if a Kassena–Yoruba–Cherokee ancestral relationship were established, a legal claim to a reservation would require a separate demonstration of legal entitlement. A possible research question would therefore be: Would proof of previously unrecognized ancestry provide a legally cognizable basis for recognition of a new political community, restoration of land, modification of tribal citizenship rules, or another form of remedy? That question belongs to legal scholarship rather than genetics alone. XVI. Government Benefits Government benefits present another distinct issue. Eligibility for federal programs associated with Indigenous peoples can depend upon factors such as: tribal citizenship; federal recognition; statutory definitions; treaty rights; residence; program-specific eligibility rules. Consequently, ancestry alone does not necessarily establish eligibility for every program. A hypothetical historical discovery would need to be translated into a legally recognized political relationship before many contemporary benefits could follow. XVII. A Falsification Framework A scientifically serious version of this hypothesis must specify what evidence could disprove it. The hypothesis should be weakened or rejected if: extensive pre-Columbian Cherokee-associated DNA lacks West African ancestry; securely dated ancient genomes consistently fit established Indigenous American population models without requiring West African ancestry; no archaeological evidence survives after systematic examination; linguistic comparison produces no statistically significant regular correspondences; alleged cognates disappear when ordinary phonological rules are applied; proposed African artifacts prove post-Columbian; proposed African crops lack secure pre-Columbian contexts; the chronological evidence contradicts the proposed migration; alternative explanations account for the evidence more successfully. This is critical. A hypothesis that cannot potentially be disproven is not a useful scientific hypothesis. XVIII. The Strongest Possible Evidence The Kassena–Yoruba–Cherokee hypothesis would become substantially more compelling if researchers discovered the following combination: 1. Pre-Columbian Cherokee-associated human remains 2. Genome-wide West African ancestry 3. West African ancestry specifically related to ancient populations ancestral to Kassena or Yoruba 4. Multiple individuals 5. Secure archaeological dating 6. Archaeological artifacts associated with West Africa 7. Linguistic correspondences involving regular sound changes 8. Corresponding grammatical structures 9. Independent historical or oral evidence 10. Replication by independent research teams No single category would necessarily settle the question. Convergent evidence from several independent fields would be dramatically more informative. XIX. Conclusion The proposition that Kassena and Yoruba populations participated in the discovery or settlement of pre-Columbian North America and contributed to the ancestry of the Cherokee is a historically controversial hypothesis that should be distinguished from established historical conclusions. The available classifications of Kasem, Yoruba, and Cherokee languages place them in different linguistic families, and existing ancient-DNA research does not presently establish a Kassena–Yoruba origin for the Cherokee. Nevertheless, the hypothesis is sufficiently specific to be tested. Its strongest test would involve the convergence of ancient DNA, archaeology, comparative linguistics, population genetics, botanical evidence, maritime archaeology, and historical documentation. The question of reparations, reservations, and government benefits must then be treated separately from the historical question. Demonstrating ancestry would not automatically establish a contemporary legal remedy. Such remedies would depend upon federal Indian law, tribal sovereignty, treaties, statutes, recognition procedures, and judicial precedent. The ultimate intellectual objective should therefore not be to begin with a predetermined legal conclusion. It should be to determine whether the historical proposition survives rigorous testing. If the evidence ultimately demonstrates no substantial Kassena or Yoruba contribution to pre-Columbian Cherokee populations, the hypothesis should be revised or abandoned. If, however, future research produces securely dated ancient genomes, archaeological evidence, linguistic correspondences, and independent historical evidence demonstrating such a connection, then the historical understanding of pre-Columbian America would require substantial revision—and the resulting questions concerning identity, political recognition, land, reparations, and governmental obligations would become legitimate subjects of serious legal and political inquiry. The central proposition is therefore best expressed as a testable historical question: Did populations ancestral to the Kassena and Yoruba cross the Atlantic before European colonization, establish enduring relationships with Indigenous populations of North America, and contribute to the ethnogenesis of the Cherokee—and, if that relationship can be demonstrated, what historical and legal consequences should follow? That question can be investigated without assuming its conclusion.
